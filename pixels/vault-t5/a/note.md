Notes from a week by the harbour

The ferry arrived a little after seven, when the quay was still empty and the lamps had not yet gone out. I carried my bag past the fish market and found the guesthouse by the smell of fresh bread drifting from its kitchen window.

My room looked over the water. In the morning the boats left one after another, each trailing a long white line, and by the time I had finished my coffee the bay was quiet again, as if nothing had ever moved across it.

On the second day I walked the old sea wall as far as the lighthouse. The keeper, a patient man with a weathered face, let me climb the stairs and showed me the lamp that his grandfather had polished every evening for forty years.

Lunch was a bowl of mussels at a table so close to the sea that the spray reached the bread basket. The waiter laughed, moved the basket, and brought a second one without being asked, which I took as the best possible welcome.

The market opens before dawn and closes before noon. I learned to arrive early, to ask the price twice, and to buy only what I could eat that day, because the stalls sell what the boats bring and the boats bring only what the weather allows.

An afternoon storm kept me indoors on the fourth day. I read the whole of a borrowed novel by the window, listening to the rain on the tiles, and when the clouds finally broke the whole town smelled of salt and wet stone.

The library is a single room above the post office, with a spiral staircase and a cat that owns the best chair. The librarian keeps a ledger of every visitor, and she asked me to write a sentence about the town before I left.

Evenings belong to the square, where old men play cards under the plane trees and children chase pigeons around the fountain. Nobody hurries, and nobody seems to mind that the church clock is always a quarter hour behind.

I spent one morning learning to mend a net from a woman who had done it since she was nine. Her fingers moved so quickly that I could not follow the knots, but she was kind enough to repeat each one slowly until mine held.

On the last night the whole street ate together under strings of small lights. There was grilled fish, a salad of tomatoes still warm from the sun, and a cake that somebody insisted was not for sale but was somehow shared with everyone.

The ferry leaves at dawn, and I have already decided to come back in autumn, when the tourists are gone and the harbour belongs once more to the people who live beside it.
